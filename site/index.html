What can actually be known about the safety of India's food

India's legal limits for pesticides, metals and toxins set beside the EU, Codex and the US; what the contamination found in Indian food means for health; and how places compare. Built only from public records, with every number traceable to its source.

India publishes no programmatic dataset of food test results. What exists is pieced together here from the regulator's rule-book, disclosures to Parliament, EU border checks, WHO and World Bank data, and open product labels.

Why the same food has different limits in different places

A pesticide limit follows from how the pesticide is allowed to be used. Regulators estimate the highest residue left when a crop is treated as the label permits, and set acceptable intakes from the toxicology alongside (FAO/WHO JMPR). A country that permits a use on a crop sets a limit that allows for it; a country that does not, sets none.

Where the EU permits no use, its limit is the detection floor. For substances it has not approved, or not approved on that food, the EU sets the limit at the lowest level a laboratory can reliably quantify, and a general 0.01 mg/kg wherever it has set nothing (Regulation (EC) 396/2005, Art. 18). Such a limit says "this should not be found", not "this level is safe", which is why most of the gaps above are large.

The US permits no residue without a tolerance, and a tolerance must give "a reasonable certainty of no harm", with an extra tenfold safety factor for children unless data support another (Food Quality Protection Act).

Codex limits are the international reference. Under the WTO's SPS Agreement, countries base their measures on them, and may be stricter where there is scientific justification (SPS Agreement, Art. 3).

Contaminants are not used on purpose (metals, mycotoxins), so their limits are set as low as reasonably achievable with good agricultural and manufacturing practice, after a risk assessment (Codex CXS 193). They reflect what a food supply can meet as well as toxicity.

Each pair in the table above carries its reason: whether the EU's value is a detection-level limit for a pesticide it does not permit on that food, its 0.01 default, or a limit for a use both permit.

How much of that food reaches a safe-intake limit?

For each recent finding on Indian food with a measured concentration: the grams a 60 kg adult could eat every day for life within the acceptable daily intake (chronic), or in one day within the acute reference dose. Guidance values from EFSA, JMPR and JECFA. Where no safe level exists (genotoxic carcinogens, or where the safe intake was withdrawn) no number is given. Classify a food-safety report

Paste a headline or complaint. Rules from the hazard knowledge base run first; a model trained on 15,210 EU notifications and tested on 6,934 later ones (91.8% accuracy) runs alongside and is used only when it is at least 60% sure. It reads what the text says; it cannot test any food. Runs in your browser.

Hazards and what they do to people

The knowledge base behind the classification: each hazard's known health outcomes, who is most at risk, the IARC cancer classification where one exists, and the source of each statement (WHO, IARC, EFSA, US EPA, CDC, FAO). It says what can happen, not how likely it is.

Products listed in Open Food Facts as sold in India: crowd-sourced label transcriptions, not lab analyses, and not a sample of what India eats. Nutri-Score and NOVA as Open Food Facts computes them; "high in" uses the UK front-of-pack thresholds per 100 g (India has no final scheme).

Sources and how far to trust them

Confidence describes whether a record states what its publisher disclosed: high for official machine-readable data, medium for validated document extraction or cited curation, low for unvalidated extraction or media. It says nothing about representativeness; each source's limits are listed with it.
