Briefing for Prof. Manu Prakash

A disease predictor needs measurements that do not exist yet

FoodSafe links food contamination to disease, using public records only. It started in India and is meant to work worldwide, for the people most at risk. The part I cannot build alone is the measurement.

Chaitanya TripathiOctober 2026Figures as of 3 Oct 2026

The chain, and where it breaks

A predictor needs every link. Two are missing and one is only partly there.

Built

Rules

56,000 legal limits from FSSAI, the EU, Codex and the US, compared side by side.

Partial

Exposure

EU border checks for 164 countries. For India, only yearly state totals.

Missing

Measurement

No district or market testing is public. The regulator's portal blocks anonymous access.

Built

Hazard to disease

A cited lookup: 52 hazards, IARC classes for 1,128 agents.

Missing

Outcomes

Nothing shows where contaminated food makes people ill.

Without measured exposure and observed illness in the same place, a trained predictor would be learning from invented data. I chose not to ship one.

What works today

Everything is public, every figure keeps its source, and nothing is synthetic.

The rules, side by side

India's limits for pesticides, metals and toxins against the EU, Codex and the US, across 1,711 food-hazard pairs. India permits more than the EU in 831. Aflatoxin M1 in milk is 0.5 µg/kg in India and 0.05 in the EU.

What gets found

32,786 EU border notifications from 164 countries. Indian food is third most flagged, with 2,228.

The disease link

Each hazard is tied to the illnesses it causes, with a source for each.

A text classifier

Sorts notifications by hazard type, 92% accurate on notifications that came after its training data. It reads text and cannot detect contamination.

Places

Indian states and 217 countries: WHO food-safety capacity, World Bank nutrition, WHO global burden (1.52 million deaths in 2021), and about 21,000 packaged foods sold in India.

Where I am stuck

India's food regulator blocks anonymous access to its recall and testing portal. The only real pass-or-fail data I found is state-level yearly totals. No public dataset links where food is contaminated to where people fall ill. A model trained to predict disease would learn from data I made up.

What the state data can predict. Nothing beat "same as last year." That may reflect how inspectors work more than how risky the food is: Uttar Pradesh fails 42 to 61 percent of samples every year, Gujarat 6 to 11.

Taking it worldwide

The people most exposed are also the people tested least: young children, pregnant women, the malnourished, anyone without clean water. First hazard: aflatoxin in maize and groundnuts across sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Sources I plan to add (licences not yet checked):

The output would be a transparent susceptibility index, with every input cited. It would not be a disease predictor.

Five questions

1

With no paired exposure and outcome data, is a predictor the right target? Or should the first product be a measurement system that creates the data?

2

Could paper-based or smartphone assays reach the sensitivity needed to test against legal limits, for example aflatoxin M1 at 0.5 against 0.05 µg/kg? Who does this well?

3

For a first validation study, which hazard, food and place would you pick, and what result would convince you it is real?

4

Who holds foodborne-illness data and would work with a student project? Is there prior work I have missed?

5

Globally, should I start with one hazard in the most vulnerable regions, or a broad index?

What would help most

A 15-minute call, or the name of one person I should talk to.
